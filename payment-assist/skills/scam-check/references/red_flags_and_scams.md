# Red flags and scam typologies

Red flags are contextual and not conclusive: one indicator alone does not mean fraud or money laundering. Several indicators together raise concern. Never label a specific counterparty a scammer; describe the pattern and how to verify.

## 1. Authorised push payment (APP) scam patterns

| Scam type | Typical signs | What the user should do |
| - | - | - |
| Investment scam | Contact via social media, messenger or dating app; promised high or "guaranteed" returns; pressure to act fast; platform not on any regulator register; small initial "profits" shown to encourage larger payments; requests to pay "taxes" or "fees" to withdraw | Check the firm on the regulator register and IOSCO I-SCAN; do not send more money to "unlock" withdrawals; contact the bank and the police |
| Romance scam | Online relationship, never met in person (or video avoided); sudden emergency, medical bills, travel costs, customs fees, investment opportunity | Do not send money to someone you have not met; talk to someone you trust |
| Advance-fee / prize / inheritance | Must pay a fee to receive a larger sum, prize, loan or inheritance | Legitimate prizes and loans do not require upfront payment to a private account |
| Invoice redirection / business email compromise | Supplier or colleague "changes bank details" by email; urgency; slightly altered email domain; CEO asks for an urgent confidential payment | Verify by phone using a number from an independent source (not from the email); compare with previous payments |
| Impersonation ("safe account") | Caller claims to be from the bank, police, tax office or a regulator; asks to move money to a "safe account" or to install an app | Banks and police never ask you to move money to protect it; hang up and call the bank on the number on your card |
| Remote-access scam | Asked to install AnyDesk, TeamViewer or similar, or to share the screen while banking | Stop the session immediately; never bank while someone else has remote access |
| Purchase scam | Online seller asks for bank transfer instead of the platform's payment; price far below market; pressure to pay quickly; seller account recently created | Use the platform's protected payment; verify the seller |
| Rental / deposit scam | Landlord abroad, cannot show the property, asks for deposit before viewing | Do not pay before viewing and signing; check the landlord's identity |
| Job / money mule recruitment | "Job" that involves receiving money and forwarding it, or opening accounts for others | Refuse; receiving and forwarding funds for others can be a criminal offence (money mule) |
| Recovery scam | Someone offers to recover money lost to a previous scam for an upfront fee | Report to police; legitimate recovery does not require upfront payment to unknown parties |
| Crypto ATM / crypto transfer demand | Asked to pay via crypto, gift cards or crypto ATM | Treat as a strong scam indicator |

## 2. Situational high-risk indicators (from supervisory fraud guidance)

- The payee was added very recently, or the payee contacted the user first.
- The user changed device, phone number or spending limit shortly before the payment.
- Someone is guiding the user step by step (on the phone or via remote access) while they make the payment.
- Pressure of time, secrecy ("don't tell your bank"), or threats.
- The name the bank shows in VoP does not match the intended recipient.
- Payment to a private person's account for what is described as a company invoice or an investment.
- The recipient's account is in a different country from where the recipient claims to be.

## 3. Money-laundering red flags relevant to the user's own payment

Explain these neutrally as things a bank may ask about, not as accusations:

- Purpose unclear or inconsistent with the relationship between the parties.
- Third-party payments: paying on behalf of someone else, or the invoice is issued by one company but payment goes to another.
- Round amounts with no document, frequent payments just below amounts that triggered questions before.
- Recipient in a high-risk or sanctioned jurisdiction, or a bank in such a jurisdiction in the chain.
- Funds arrive and are immediately sent onward (pass-through).
- Goods that do not fit the business of the buyer or seller; prices far from market value (trade-based money laundering).
- Use of shell companies, nominee directors, very recently incorporated companies.
- Crypto purchases immediately after receiving funds from unknown sources.

## 4. Standard awareness messages (translate to the user's language)

- **Irreversibility**: "Bank transfers, especially instant ones, are very difficult or impossible to reverse once sent. Make sure you know and trust the recipient."
- **New payee**: "Before you send money to a new payee, verify their details through a separate channel you trust, especially for online purchases or unfamiliar sellers."
- **Unsolicited contact**: "If the recipient contacted you first with an investment, prize, romance or loan offer, be very careful: this is how most payment scams start."
- **Pressure**: "Legitimate businesses and banks do not pressure you to pay immediately or ask you to keep a payment secret."
- **Crypto**: "Crypto payments are irreversible and a common target for scams. Never send crypto to someone who contacted you first."

## 5. If the user may already be a victim

1. Contact your bank immediately using the number on your card or the official website, and ask them to try to recall the payment.
2. Report to the police (keep screenshots, messages, transaction details).
3. Report to the national financial regulator or consumer protection authority if an investment firm was involved.
4. Change passwords, remove any remote-access apps, and enable two-factor authentication.
5. Be cautious of "recovery" offers.
